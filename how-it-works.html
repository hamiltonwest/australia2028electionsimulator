How it works

The simulator plays out the next federal election thousands of times, seat by seat, and adds up the winners. How often each result comes up tells you how likely it is.

Each simulated election

  1. Starting point. Every seat starts from its 2025 result, blended with the DemosAU seat-by-seat poll (August–September 2026) and moved to match the current average of national polls.
  2. Your changes. Anything you set in the sandbox (vote shifts, leaders, events, demographic swings, local factors) is added on top. Demographic swings use each seat's 2021 Census profile (35 measures, from age and income to religion, ancestry and jobs) and move each party in line with how its 2025 vote varied across those communities.
  3. Random error. Polls miss, and they miss in patterns, so each run adds a national error plus state, regional and seat-level errors.
  4. The count. Each seat is counted the way the AEC does it: the lowest candidates drop out and their preferences flow on, based on 2025 flows, until someone has a majority.

Reading the results

  • Chances: the share of runs ending in a Labor majority, a Coalition majority or a hung parliament (76 seats wins a majority).
  • Map and central forecast: each seat goes to the party that wins it most often.
  • Ranges: the middle 90% of runs.

The Senate

Each state's Senate seats are filled with a party-level quota count using estimated preference flows. Sitting senators are assumed to keep their 2022 ticket order.

Results night and the news story

The live count replays the central result seat by seat in the order seats would be called. XYZ News, its reporters and its studio guests are fictional, and the story is written automatically from each run. It contains no real quotes.

Factions and new leaders

Labor and Liberal MPs and senators are tagged with the faction they are reported to belong to; the Nationals have no formal factions, so they are grouped by state branch. If a leader loses their seat or their party loses badly, the new leader is the most senior figure still in Parliament from the largest faction left standing. Parties without factions turn to their most senior figure left.

Limits

It can't know about candidates who haven't been chosen, local campaigns or future boundary changes, and the effects of leaders and events are judgement calls. Treat it as a guide to what could happen, not a prediction.

Checked against 2025

A forecast is only as good as its counting. This test feeds the model the actual 2025 national first preferences (and nothing from the 2026 seat poll), runs it, and compares each seat's most likely winner with who actually won in 2025. It checks the preference flows and the seat-by-seat count, not the polls.

Known weaknesses

  • Polls move together. Each run shares a national polling error across seats, but real polling misses can be bigger and more lopsided than any one run allows (2019 is the reminder).
  • Preference flows are estimates. They are fitted to 2025 and vary by seat type, but a party's voters can behave very differently when it becomes a contender, as One Nation now is.
  • New parties have no history. Community Strong and any party standing in new seats start from judgement-based figures, not results.
  • Candidates matter and are not modelled. Retirements, scandals and star recruits can move a seat by many points.
  • The Coalition is one vote. Liberal, National, LNP and CLP seats are shown separately, but the model treats the Coalition vote as a single bloc unless you split it.
  • The Senate is simpler than the real count. It counts parties, not individual candidates, and uses estimated flows. It reproduces all 40 seats won in 2025, but only after a one-point calibration in New South Wales; Lidia Thorpe's and Tammy Tyrrell's starting votes are judgement, as neither was on the 2025 ballot.
